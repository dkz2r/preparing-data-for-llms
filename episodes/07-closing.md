## Closing

This material set out to answer one question: where does the enormous amount of suitable text a language model needs come from, and what does *suitable* even mean. Four parts on, both halves have an answer. Where the text comes from is now concrete: curated and web sources, drawn along the two paths of Part 1 and reduced, cheaply and in the right order, to the clean fraction worth keeping. What *suitable* means turned out to be the harder finding: bad text can be defined up front by the tells you can count, but good text has none, so it can only be approximated by a proxy and confirmed after the fact.

Read back through the parts, here is what each was meant to leave you with:

| Part | What you learned | How to apply it |
|----|----|----|
| **1) The pipeline** | Turn curated and web text into one clean, shared record, and keep that record *traceable*. | Save the metadata and intermediate outputs as you go. |
| **2 Working at scale** | At web scale every step is also a compute decision; wall-clock time, tied to the project’s compute window, is the budget that binds. | Run the cheap, decisive cuts first and the expensive comparisons last. |
| **3) The quality question** | Cheap surface heuristics judge a document’s *form* but stay blind to its *value*; reading value means paying for a model. | Never ask a surface filter whether text is *worth* learning from; it knows only the shape. |
| **4) Cheap proxies for good text** | Good text cannot be defined outright, so every quality signal is a cheap proxy you can confirm only after training. | Treat a quality score as a hypothesis to test downstream, not a ground truth to trust up front. |

A language model is a product of its training data, so every decision taken before training, what to keep, what to call good, what to discard, is a decision about what the model can become. None of it shows in the finished weights, yet all of it is already there.

**What we did not cover**

This material stops at preparing crawled and curated text. The biggest neighbor it leaves out is **synthetic data**, text generated by a model rather than collected, which sidesteps the crawl entirely but brings its own quality and diversity questions (see this [survey of best practices](https://arxiv.org/abs/2404.07503)). Tokenization and the *mixing weights* that set how much of each source a model sees are likewise their own topics.

A closing word on where to take this. The field moves quickly, and the specifics will date faster than the principles: the libraries turn over and the judges keep improving. What carries forward is the way of thinking, keep the data traceable, match each step to its budget, and treat every quality signal as a proxy you still have to check. Hold on to those, and the next tool will be easy to learn.
